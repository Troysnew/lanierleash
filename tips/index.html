Smart, local dog tips

Hot Pavement and Heat in Georgia Summers

Walk early or late, stick to grass, and never leave your dog in a parked car.

Georgia summers are long and humid, and sidewalks, roads and parking lots soak up the sun. Hot pavement can burn your dog's paw pads. On very hot days, keep walks short, walk on grass when you can, and save longer outings for early morning or evening.

Heat can also cause heatstroke. Watch for heavy panting, trouble breathing, drooling, weakness, stumbling or collapse. Flat-faced dogs like pugs and bulldogs can't cool off by panting as well, so they are at higher risk. Older dogs, overweight dogs and dogs with heart or lung problems also need extra care.

Always bring fresh water and find shade on hikes, ball games and lake days. Never leave your dog alone in a parked car, even for a quick stop. A car heats up fast, and it can be deadly.

If you think your dog is overheating, move them into shade or air conditioning, offer water, and call your vet or an emergency clinic right away. Heatstroke can turn serious quickly.

Ticks and Tick-Borne Disease in Georgia

Lone star, American dog and black-legged ticks all live here. Use year-round prevention and check your dog after every outing.

Ticks are a fact of life around Lake Lanier and the north Georgia woods. The most common ones here are the lone star tick, the American dog tick and the black-legged (deer) tick. They can carry diseases such as Rocky Mountain spotted fever, ehrlichiosis, anaplasmosis and Lyme disease. Lone star ticks are especially common in brushy, low areas where deer live.

The Companion Animal Parasite Council recommends tick control for dogs all year, every year, not just in summer. Ask your vet which product fits your dog, and follow the label exactly. Home remedies rarely work.

After hikes, trail runs or time in tall grass, check your dog from nose to tail. Look closely between the toes, in and around the ears, and under the collar. To remove a tick, use fine-tipped tweezers, grab it close to the skin, and pull with a slow, steady motion. Don't twist or jerk. Clean the spot and wash your hands.

At home, keep the grass cut short. If your dog seems tired, limps or loses their appetite after a tick bite, call your vet.

Lake Lanier Water Safety for Dogs

Dogs aren't allowed on Corps swim beaches. Keep your dog on a leash under 6 feet in parks and a dog life jacket on the water.

Lake Lanier is a great place for dogs, but it has some rules worth knowing. At U.S. Army Corps of Engineers parks, pets must be on a leash shorter than six feet. Pets are not allowed on designated swimming beaches, in restrooms or on playgrounds. Service animals are the exception. Please pick up after your dog in all public areas.

There are no lifeguards at Lanier, and Corps beaches warn of deep drop-offs past the swim line. Around islands and rocky shores, the bottom can fall away fast, and you may find rocks or fallen trees under the water. Not every dog is a strong swimmer, and even good swimmers get tired. A well-fitted dog life jacket with a handle on the back makes it much easier to lift your dog back into a boat.

Boat traffic can be heavy, especially on summer weekends and holidays. Keep your dog close and out of busy channels, watch for boat wakes, and bring fresh drinking water so they don't drink the lake. Rinse your dog off after swimming.

Toxic Algae and Water Quality Alerts on Lanier

If the water looks like pea soup or spilled paint, keep your dog out and rinse them off if they get in.

Harmful algal blooms, sometimes called toxic algae, can show up in Georgia lakes during hot weather. They can make dogs very sick, because dogs swim in the water, drink it and lick their fur afterward.

Georgia EPD says a bloom may look like bright green, blue, brown or red water. It can also look like pea soup, spilled green paint, or a thick floating scum. Dead or stressed fish are another warning sign.

If you see any of this, keep your dog out of the water and don't play fetch nearby. If your dog does touch it, wash their fur well with clean water before they can lick themselves. Call your vet right away if your dog vomits, has diarrhea, has trouble breathing, or acts wobbly or strange after a swim.

Swimming advisories come from Georgia DNR or the lake manager, which at Lanier is the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers. Check for posted signs before you swim. To report a suspected bloom, contact the lake manager, or send Georgia EPD the location, date, time and photos.

Leptospirosis Risk From Lakes and Puddles

Lepto spreads through water and soil contaminated by animal urine. Ask your vet about the vaccine.

Leptospirosis is a bacterial disease that can make dogs, and people, seriously ill. The bacteria spread through the urine of infected animals like rodents and wildlife. They can live in water or soil for weeks to months, especially in slow-moving or standing water like ponds and puddles.

The CDC says almost every dog is at some risk. The risk is higher for dogs that swim, hike, or roam where wildlife live, and for dogs that spend time at dog parks, boarding or daycare.

There is a vaccine for dogs. It doesn't protect against every strain, but it is still recommended for many dogs. Ask your vet whether it makes sense for your dog's lifestyle.

You can also lower the risk at home. Bring fresh water on walks so your dog doesn't drink from puddles or ponds, and keep rodents away from your house and garage.

If your dog seems sick after time in the water or woods, call your vet soon, because early treatment helps. If your dog is diagnosed, wear gloves when cleaning up urine and wash your hands well.

Leash and Dog-at-Large Rules in Forsyth, Hall and Gwinnett

In all three counties, your dog must be under control whenever it is off your property.

Every county around Lake Lanier has rules against letting dogs roam. The details vary a little, but the idea is the same: when your dog is off your property, it needs to be under your control.

In Forsyth County, a dog off its owner's property must be on a leash held by a capable person, inside a vehicle, or in a proper enclosure. Dogs three months and older must also wear a collar with a current rabies tag. A microchip doesn't replace the tag. The owner can be cited even if they didn't know the dog got loose.

Gwinnett County's ordinance treats any dog off a leash, not at heel and not beside a capable person, and not confined on its owner's property as running at large. Animal control can impound loose dogs.

Hall County Animal Control enforces the county's animal ordinances and responds to loose or uncontrolled dogs. If you see a loose dog in Hall, you can call Animal Control on weekdays.

City rules and park rules can add more, so check signs at parks and trails. And always carry bags to clean up after your dog.

Georgia's Rabies Vaccination Law

Georgia law requires dogs and cats to be vaccinated for rabies by a licensed vet. Keep the tag and certificate handy.

Rabies is still found in Georgia wildlife, and local health officials post alerts when a raccoon, fox, bat or stray cat tests positive. That is one reason Georgia law requires dogs, cats and ferrets to be vaccinated against rabies by a licensed veterinarian.

After the first shot, a booster is due one year later. After that, your dog gets boosters every one or three years, depending on the vaccine your vet uses and your county's rules. In Forsyth County, every dog and cat three months or older must be vaccinated, and dogs must wear a current rabies tag on their collar.

Keep your rabies certificate somewhere easy to find. You will often need it for boarding, groomers, daycare and county dog parks.

If your dog tangles with a wild or stray animal, call your vet and your county animal services right away, even if your dog is up to date. If a person is bitten or scratched, wash the wound with soap and water and call a doctor. Teach kids never to touch wild or stray animals.

Holiday Food and Plant Hazards

Skip the turkey bones and fatty scraps, and keep mistletoe, holly and poinsettias out of reach.

Thanksgiving and Christmas bring lots of food, guests and decorations, and some of them can hurt your dog.

At the table, skip the fatty scraps, spicy dishes and sweets. Turkey and chicken bones can splinter, so keep them away from your dog, and lock the trash can lid after dinner. Don't leave alcoholic drinks where a curious nose can reach them. Keeping your dog on their usual food is the kindest choice.

Some holiday plants are toxic to pets, including mistletoe, holly and poinsettias. Lilies are especially dangerous for cats. Snow globes can contain antifreeze, which is poisonous. Watch for broken ornaments, chewed light cords and batteries. Secure your Christmas tree so it can't tip, and block access to the tree water.

Ask guests to keep medicines zipped away in a closed cabinet, not in a purse or suitcase on the floor. A quiet room can help a nervous dog during a busy party.

If you think your dog ate something harmful, call your vet or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center at (888) 426-4435 right away.

Fireworks and Thunderstorm Anxiety

Keep your dog inside during fireworks, double-check ID tags and microchips, and talk to your vet before July 4th.

Summer around Lake Lanier means pop-up thunderstorms and lots of fireworks, especially over the water on the Fourth of July. Many dogs are scared of loud booms, and frightened dogs can bolt, dig under fences or slip their collars.

Before the holiday, make sure your dog wears a collar with ID tags and that their microchip is registered with your current phone number. If your dog does get loose, that is the fastest way to get them home.

Keep your dog indoors while fireworks are going off. Leave them at home rather than taking them to a lakeside show or out on a boat. A quiet room with the blinds closed, a favorite bed or crate, and some background noise like a fan or TV can help.

If your dog shakes, pants, hides or panics during storms or fireworks, talk to your vet ahead of time. They can suggest training tips, calming tools or medicine that may help. Don't give your dog any human medicine unless your vet says it's okay.

Bringing Your Dog to Restaurant Patios

In Georgia, dogs are allowed only on approved outdoor patios. Keep them leashed, on the ground, and out of the food.

Lots of restaurants around Cumming, Alpharetta and Gainesville welcome dogs on their patios. Georgia rules set some limits, and good manners help keep those patios dog-friendly.

Pet dogs are allowed only in outdoor dining areas that the local health department has approved for dogs. Your dog has to get to the patio from outside, not through the dining room. Only service animals can go inside. If you aren't sure, ask the host before you sit down.

Keep your dog on a leash and on the ground, not on chairs, tables or your lap. Dogs aren't allowed to eat on the patio, so leave the treats and the plate-sharing for home. Many places will bring a disposable bowl of water, set away from the tables.

Pick a quiet corner, and only bring a dog who can settle down calmly around strangers, other dogs and dropped food. If your dog starts barking, whining or getting restless, it's fine to call it a day and take your meal to go. Clean up any accidents right away and thank your server.
